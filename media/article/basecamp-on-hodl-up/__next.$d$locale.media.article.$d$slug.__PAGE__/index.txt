Happy to be here, good to meet y'all. I was looking you guys up, doing some background research, and I think this is going to be a fun conversation.</p><p><strong>Atis Shala:</strong> Definitely. Considering you joined crypto in 2013, if I'm not mistaken, this is going to be a long one. Before we get into everything else, I want to ask you the question I ask everyone: what got you into crypto? What was the idea behind you joining? It's a whole world out there, and crypto has changed so much over the years.</p><p><strong>Corey Petty:</strong> I might take up our whole time with this one. I originally did a PhD in computational physics, and I've always been fascinated with computation. My PhD was in high-performance computing, so solving really hard computational problems was something I cared about. I've also always been a deconstructionist: I like taking things apart and putting them back together with as few pieces as possible.</p><p>By happenstance, I saw a TED talk about Bitcoin while I was doing my graduate studies, and I was fascinated by it. I liked the computational problem it was solving, the Byzantine Generals' Problem, and I thought the solution was elegant. So I researched it, wanted to understand the ins and outs, wanted to see how it could break down. Then I started playing with it, and I learned about money and the weirdness that is money. I found it fascinating that we'd built real, sovereign internet money, something we'd never had before. It was a fundamentally new thing to me, and I got lost in it.</p><p>I started mining because the best way to learn is by doing, and the more competent I became, the more fascinated I got. Eventually, I started a podcast and met a lot of people through great conversations – that's The Bitcoin Podcast… shameless plug. I really enjoyed it, and eventually I left academia, because the problems I was solving there, while interesting, were problems nobody cared about. Meanwhile, this huge industry was emerging that I saw as an exponential technology about to take off, one that gives power back to people. I care a lot about cryptography, information, and decentralised systems, so I got a job doing data science and blockchain education, then moved into security.</p><p>Then I got a job at Status after their ICO, where I was lead security engineer and CSO. I kept doing it because the ecosystem kept exploding, and I was always trying to build things I believe in: resilient systems built for adversarial settings, resistant to being taken over. That's core to who I am: figuring out how to build systems that aren't asymmetric in power.</p><p><strong>Dren Hima:</strong> I resonate with that. When we joined crypto, we were drawn in by the same philosophy. You've lived through the most important cycles this space has seen and watched it evolve into what it is today. Do you like it? Did you predict this is where it would end up? It doesn't feel the same anymore. I get nostalgic about a lot of things, but especially about when we first joined crypto. We can talk all day about ideology and institutions, but it just doesn't feel the same. I want your take, because I know you've been critical of the institutionalisation of crypto.</p><p><strong>Corey Petty:</strong> No, I don't like it, but I'm not surprised by it either. The ideology isn't gone. When we started The Bitcoin Podcast, our whole line was "adoption is the only thing that matters." We were going to replace the banks, give people ownership of their own financial data. But that's not the adoption we got, because it's genuinely hard to do. Using a wallet, taking responsibility for your own private keys, understanding what you're signing, understanding what chain your tokens are on, navigating the weird dynamics of DeFi – all of that is hard, and people don't like hard. It means learning something new and changing their behaviour.</p><p>So the adoption we got was driven by scale and convenience, and a lot of what we built ended up being the same thing we already had, just on the internet: traditional finance, online. That isn't what I signed up for, but it's clearly the direction things went, because it's what people wanted. We haven't yet convinced them of the thing I got into this for, and why they need it. I think we're getting there. People are starting to realise this isn't fundamentally different from what came before. I'm still hopeful we can build something that actually feels better than what we already have.</p><p><strong>Atis Shala:</strong> On the institutionalisation of crypto, I think it was inevitable, like you said. But I also think we asked for it, in a way. Maybe not you specifically, but the majority of the industry. Back in 2020 and 2021, whenever there was news of some new institution getting into crypto, everyone got fired up. I think the industry has an identity crisis around this, and it connects to privacy too, because I think anonymity and privacy in crypto should be non-negotiable.</p><p><strong>Corey Petty:</strong> I'd agree.</p><p><strong>Atis Shala:</strong> But how realistic is that? Regular people don't really care, do they? If you ask my parents whether they care that their data gets sold from their social media accounts to feed algorithms that essentially enslave them digitally, they wouldn't care. I try to say I care, and I try to stand behind the movement, but it's hard to stay idealistic in today's world. How would you convince someone to fight this fight? I see it almost as a war: How do you get people to stand behind these values?</p><p><strong>Corey Petty:</strong> That's a hard one. Honestly, I don't think you convince most people directly. This mirrors something I saw doing security work: people only care about security once it's already been breached, once it personally affects them, and by then it's usually too late. So, as builders, we have to be the ideological ones and build things that don't give people the option to skip these protections. It has to be built into the substrate, the infrastructure itself, so people are protected by default and have to actively opt out if they want to. That's consent, versus the current model, where consent was never really given.</p><p>Privacy is the same story. The transparency baked into everything we use today creates a surveillance economy, and the easiest way to profit from that is to aggregate data and exploit it; the user becomes the product. Companies aren't building for what users need; they're building to take advantage of them, because that's what the current substrate incentivises. So I'm less interested in trying to "sell" privacy and anonymity, and more interested in building a substrate that doesn't give people that choice to be exploited by default, one where they have to actively consent, and in some cases can even profit from their own data instead of someone else scraping it because that's the cheapest option.</p><p>You can't bolt privacy onto a system after the fact – or you can, but it's much harder. The lifecycle of a transaction has many steps, and if you think of the blockchain as the settlement layer, that's the end of the lifecycle. Almost all the surveillance and profiteering happens before you ever get there. So it doesn't matter if you have a privacy solution on the blockchain if everything's already been harvested upstream, or if bad actors just go after whoever's using the privacy solution instead. You have to secure and privatise the entire transaction lifecycle, so the user can choose when to disclose information, instead of having it sold off before they ever had a choice.</p><p><strong>Dren Hima:</strong> That's exactly it.
